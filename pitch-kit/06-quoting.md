# How to put a number on it

The fixed price is the promise the whole business rests on. This is how to reach one you will not
resent in week three.

## The arithmetic

**Two different things get confused, so keep them apart:**

- **Effort days** — actual working days of your time. This is what you price on.
- **Elapsed time** — what the client sees on the calendar. Longer, because you have a full-time
  job and because parallel-running takes a fortnight of waiting, not working.

The published bands assume roughly ₹12,000 per effort day:

| Band | Effort days | Price | Elapsed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quick win | 1.5 – 3.5 | ₹18,000 – ₹45,000 | 3–7 working days |
| Connected module | 5 – 15 | ₹60,000 – ₹1,80,000 | 2–4 weeks |
| System | 20+ | from ₹2,50,000 | staged, scheduled |

## The estimate

**1. List the steps.** Not "build the report" — read the files, handle the three date formats,
handle the amount formats, de-duplicate, aggregate, render, schedule, alert on failure, document,
hand over. Ten small things you can estimate beat one big thing you cannot.

**2. Estimate each one in hours, honestly and optimistically.** You will be wrong; that is fine,
because of step 3.

**3. Multiply by 2.** Not because you are slow. Because of the things that are not building:

- The export that turns out not to contain the field you assumed
- Access that takes four days to arrange
- The rule nobody mentioned because everybody knows it
- Running it in parallel and reconciling a difference of ₹340
- Writing the handover notes
- The two rounds of "can it also…"

If you have never built against this system before, multiply by 2.5.

**4. Convert to days, then to a price.** Round *up* to a sensible number. ₹38,000 not ₹37,400 —
a price to the hundred rupee looks like a bill, not a quote.

**5. Sanity-check against payback.** Run their numbers through `[SITE]/calculator`. If the build
does not pay for itself inside about a year, either the scope is too big or the job was not worth
automating — and you should have found that in the audit.

## When to quote the top of the band

- Their systems are unfamiliar to you
- More than two systems are involved
- The data is genuinely messy, not just untidy
- Anything touching money, payroll, or compliance — the cost of being wrong is theirs, and the
  care you take is real work
- Multiple stakeholders have to agree on the rules
- They need it faster than your normal turnaround

## When to quote the bottom

- You have built something close before and can reuse the shape
- One clean system with a decent export
- One decision-maker
- It is your first client, and a case study is worth more than ₹8,000

## Never do this

**Do not discount to win the work.** Once you are the person who drops 20% when pushed, every
future quote is a starting position. Use the free-audit offer for the first five clients instead —
it costs you a half-day, is a genuine gift, and leaves the price list intact.

**Do not quote on a phone call.** "I'll put it in writing by Thursday" is always the right answer.
Numbers said out loud are remembered as smaller than they were.

**Do not swallow scope creep to be liked.** "That's a good idea — it's outside what we agreed,
but it's about ₹8,000 and two days. Want me to add it?" said cheerfully, the first time, sets the
pattern for the whole engagement. Saying nothing the first time makes the fourth request
impossible to refuse.

**Do not quote a job you have not watched.** Every under-priced build in history started with
"it's basically just…".

## Raising your prices

Put them up when: you have turned work away twice, or a build came in well under estimate twice
in a row, or your waiting list is longer than three weeks.

Raise the *bands* on the website, not the quote in front of a client. Existing clients keep their
price until their next new job. Nobody is ever told "the price went up because I'm busier", even
though that is exactly the reason and a perfectly good one.
