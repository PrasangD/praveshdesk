# The discovery call

Fifteen minutes, free, and the only goal is to find **one job** worth auditing. Not to sell. Not
to demo everything. One job.

If you leave the call with a job named, its hours estimated, and who does it — the call worked,
whether or not they bought anything.

## Before the call (five minutes)

- Look at their website: what do they sell, how many locations, how many people.
- Write down one guess at a job they probably do by hand. You will be wrong half the time, but
  the guess makes the first question specific.
- Have `[SITE]/demo` open in a tab. Do not open it unless it is useful.

## The order of questions

**1. What the business actually does** *(2 min — you are listening for volume and repetition)*

> "Tell me what [COMPANY] does day to day. Not the website version — what actually happens?"

**2. Find the job** *(4 min — the most important part of the call)*

> "Is there anything somebody does every week that they'd be glad never to do again?"

If that gets nothing, get specific. One of these usually lands:

> - "Who puts the monthly figures together, and where do they come from?"
> - "Is there anything you type into two different systems?"
> - "What do you produce a lot of, one at a time? Invoices, certificates, reports?"
> - "What's the last thing that went wrong because somebody missed a step?"
> - "What happens when [the person who does it] is on leave?"

That last one is the best question in the kit. The answer is often a small panic, and a panic is
a budget.

**3. Size it** *(3 min — these four numbers are the whole qualification)*

> - "How long does it take, roughly, each time?"
> - "How often?"
> - "How many people touch it?"
> - "What happens if it's wrong — who notices, and when?"

Write the numbers down. Read them back. If it is under two hours a week, say so now:

> "Honestly, at an hour a week this probably isn't worth automating yet. Is there something bigger?"

Saying that is the single most effective move available to you.

**4. Check it is buildable** *(3 min)*

> - "Where does the information live now — a system, a sheet, email, paper?"
> - "Can that system export a file, or is it locked?"
> - "Are the rules always the same, or does it depend on judgement?"
> - "Is this process about to change anyway?"

A yes to that last one means stop. Automating a process a month before it changes wastes their
money and your reputation.

**5. Who decides** *(1 min)*

> "If this were worth doing, who'd need to agree to it besides you?"

**6. Close to the audit, not to the build** *(2 min)*

> "Here's what I'd suggest. I come in for half a day, watch that job being done and look at two or
> three others while I'm there, and send you a written report: what each one costs you a year,
> what's worth automating, what isn't, and a price for each. That's ₹9,999, and it comes off the
> first build if you go ahead. If the report says nothing's worth doing, you've still got the
> report and I've still told you the truth."

Then stop talking.

## What to do with an objection

Answer it in one sentence and go back to the job. Do not defend the business model — the material
in `07-objections.md` covers the nine things you will actually be asked.

## After the call, same day

Send a short email. This is the one that gets you the audit:

> Hi [NAME],
>
> Thanks for the time. To make sure I understood:
>
> - **The job:** [one sentence, their words not yours]
> - **Who does it:** [name/role], about [X] hours a week
> - **Where the data is:** [systems]
> - **What goes wrong:** [what they said]
>
> On those numbers that's roughly [X × 52] hours a year, which at a rough loaded cost is about
> ₹[figure] a year. You can check that yourself here: [SITE]/calculator
>
> If you want me to look at it properly, the audit is ₹9,999 and credited against the first build.
> I could come on [two specific dates].
>
> And if the answer's no, no problem at all — the calculator is useful on its own.
>
> [YOUR NAME]

**Why it works:** it proves you listened, it does the arithmetic for them, it gives two dates
rather than "let me know", and it gives them an easy exit — which makes taking the next step feel
like their decision rather than yours.

## Red flags — walk away politely

- They want a fixed price today, without letting you see the work.
- "Can you build us something like [large SaaS product]?"
- The process is changing next quarter.
- They will not let you talk to the person who actually does the job.
- They want the automation but not the conversation about how the work really happens.
- Anything on your own "will not build" list.

Say no on the call. A bad first client costs more than an empty month.
